History of Frutiger Aero

This section is a brief explanation of the history of the Frutiger Aero aesthetic, from what led to its rise in popularity, up to its downfall and eventual revival on social media. For a quick explanation of the term Frutiger Aero, jump to this section.

Introduction to Frutiger Aero

Before we delve into the history of Frutiger Aero, we need to understand what it is even referring to. The term, coined in 2017, is used to define or categorize a certain aesthetic that was present from approximately 2005 up to 2013 in graphic design, internet aesthetics, user interfaces, and other media.

Frutiger Aero Website by Skyweaver

The name of the aesthetic was coined by the Consumer Aesthetics Research Institute, a collective of researchers and designers that are specialized in categorizing consumer aesthetics from the 1970s onwards.

The word "Frutiger" comes from the typefaces of the same name designed by Adrian Frutiger, a Swiss typeface designer responsible for fonts commonly seen in airports, transportation, and train stations. Those fonts would inspire computer fonts during the 2000s, notably Segoe UI which was used in Windows Vista onwards, Podium Sans, and Myriad Pro, and other humanistic fonts. The second part of the aesthetic's name "Aero" refers to the design guidelines for Windows Vista and Windows 7, which symbolizes the common design patterns which will be common in tech in that era.

Outside of computer operating systems, the aesthetic can be identified in game consoles such as the PS VITA, the PSP, PS3, Xbox 360 (Blades dashboard), Wii, Wii U, and some others. It can also be seen in toys such as the i-Dog from Sega, some architectural projects, like Poste Italiane in 2007, McDonald's flagship restaurant by SHH, London, Amgen Inc., in South Francisco and O2 Flagship Store in Munich's Marienplatz to count a few. It can also be seen in iPhone and Android who incorporated skeuomorphism, and Asian stock imagery, among other things.

On the web, during the middle 2000s to early 2010s, glossy interfaces that incorporated reflections, gradient, and skeuomorphism were often seen. At the time, these interfaces as used on websites were referred to as "Web 2.0", but it is also called "Web 2.0 Gloss" in the modern day when referencing to Frutiger Aero, although I could not find proof of it being called this in those days.

Evolution of Computer Interfaces (2001-2005)

Design trends come and go, and each decade had its own distinct look and feel. With the birth of World Wide Web, techology would grow to take a completely different importance in our lives.

At the start of the new millennium, thanks to the progress of technology in the sector of computing, operating system manufacturers such as Microsoft and Apple could experiment with more innovative graphical user interfaces that would make using computers easier to use and more pleasing than ever before.

At the same time, the arrival of broadband and high-speed internet, websites could implement interfaces that could "wow" people with advanced graphics, often utilizing the capabilities of Adobe Flash, unlike the static websites of the 90s.

Windows XP Luna Theme (2001)

In 2001 Windows XP by Microsoft came to the scene with the Luna theme, which was a more friendly and playful look in contrast to the previous 9x iterations of Windows with their gray, flat and simple interfaces, as it featured a vibrant blue accent color on the default theme and used gradients.

It was accompanied by the "Bliss" Wallpaper, originally titled "Bucolic Green Hills", it is a photograph of a vineyard that was cleared due to a phylloxera infestation taken in 1996 by Charles O'Rear. The photograph displays roling hills with a clear blue sky with mountains far in the background. It almost looks utopian, unreal, or dream-like.

The widespread use of Windows XP with Bliss as the default wallpaper in the years to come probably contributed in part to popularizing the use of nature and greenery in tech, advertisements, posters, etc. that would come in the following years.

More importantly, this operating system showed an effort to appear more friendly and welcoming to the average user, providing a more comforting environment to discover a new digital world. However, the interface of Windows XP was not without its critics, who called its Luna design style "Fisher-Price" looking and toy-like. Still, XP would be Microsoft's longest lasting operating system, only reaching end of life in 2014.

Mac OS X 10.0 (2001)

A few months before Windows XP in 2001, Apple had released their own new unique interface in Mac OS X 10.0, introducing the Aqua design style, with elements of the interface mimicking the effect of water droplets, and a use of reflection effects and translucency. As indicated by its name, Aqua was based on the theme of water. Its first appearance in a commercial product was in July 2000 with the release of iMovie 2.

A very innovative and realistic interface for its time, it would inspire other manufacturers such as Microsoft to pursue similar-looking projects in the future.

Both Apple and Microsoft tried to appeal to the public with pleasing, beautiful interfaces that took full advantage of newer computer graphic capabilities, often mimicking real life elements. This was following the trend of the time of early CGI and graphics becoming more and more impressive to "wow" customers.

Unlike the 90s, when interfaces and tech looked more gray and silvery (retrospectively called the Y2K era), things started to look more colorful. There was genuine optimism and excitement at the time in the general public about the possibilities offered by technology and its possible benefits on humanity and it was certainly reflected in the media of the time.

Development of Windows Aero (2005-2009)

Longhorn build 5048 (2005)

Microsoft's next operating system, codenamed "Longhorn" was planned to release sometime late in 2003, originally intended to be a minor release between Windows XP (codenamed "Whistler") and a bigger release codenamed "Blackcomb", Longhorn grew in scope until it became the next major release of Windows instead. While Whistler and Blackcomb are large ski resorts in British Columbia, Longhorn is the name of a bar between the two mountains that Whistler's visitors pass to reach Blackcomb.

The ambitions for the project grew constantly, causing numerous delays and a bloated codebase. The situation became so bad that in the summer of 2004 the project was reset, discarding years of hard work from engineers. The project would start fresh with the Windows Server 2003 codebase as a new foundation, which was also used in some 64-bit releases of Windows XP.

Windows Vista (2007)

Longhorn would eventually be renamed to "Vista" for the final release. An unprecedented beta-test program ensued involving hundreds of thousands of volunteers and companies. Between September 2005 and October 2006, Microsoft released regular Community Technology Previews (CTP) to beta testers and two release candidates to the general public.

The post-reset build 5048 of Windows Longhorn would introduce a new visual interface design, called "AERO", standing for Authentic, Energetic, Reflective, and Open.

While many parts of the user interface remained the same as previous iterations of Windows, it would incorporate a new glassy look, with reflections, and a more 3D look. Cleartype anti-aliasing was now enabled across all surfaces by default. Development of Windows Vista concluded with the November 8, 2006 announcement of its completion by co-president of Windows development, Jim Allchin.

When Windows Vista was finally released to the public in 2007, it was seen for many as a disapointement. Many of the promised features of Longhorn were discarded, and what was left was not deemed "game-changing" enough for most people to switch from XP. The new Aero interface was very demanding on the hardware of the time, and many computers that did not meet the minimum requirements were branded with the "Vista Capable" stickers. This led many customers to return to Windows XP due to bad performance.

Adding insult to injury, it also had many bugs and driver issues on release, many peripherals and software that worked fine on XP would break on Vista. The combination of problems and the bad timing led to the failure of the operating system.

The Mojave Experiment (2008)

Microsoft would try to do damage control with their advertising campaign called "Mojave Experiment". It was an effort to change what they felt like was an unfair negative consumer perception of their operating system. It involved participants that had a negative view of Vista (without having tried it) being shown a demonstration of a new operating system which they called "Mojave", which participants thought was awesome. At the end of the experiment, it would be revealed that Mojave was actually Windows Vista, much to their surprise. Were the reactions cherry-picked only to include the good ones? Probably.

However, it's true that after service packs and some updates, Windows Vista fixed many of the issues that caused it to be hated and became very usable. However, most people did not know this and Vista's horrible reputation would not improve despite Microsoft's efforts.

Windows 7 (2009)

Redemption for Microsoft came a few years later in 2009, with Windows 7. The name comes from the major version number of Windows Vista (6.0) being incremented by one.

Contrary to popular belief, the previous codenames of Blackcomb and/or Vienna refer to an earlier effort intended to succeed Windows Vista, which was canceled in early 2007 due to time and feature constraints, and was instead replaced with the Windows 7 project.

In every aspect an improvement over Vista, the new operating system focused on providing a familiar yet improved and more polished experience.

While very similar to Vista, Windows 7 brings many enhancements to the desktop experience. Notably the Taskbar receiving a major overhaul. The Windows Aero user interface has also received many enhancements. Windows 7 finally surpassed Windows XP in market share in April 2011.

Dot-com bubble and Web 2.0

Apple Website in 2001

The dot-com bubble burst had bankrupted many companies and famous websites around 2000 to to 2002. But the web did not disappear, it started to slowly recover and evolve in different ways.

Faster internet speeds with the arrival of broadband, DSL, cable, and other technologies also meant that website design could get more advanced and "flashy", in contrary to the limited graphics that dial-up limited the web to. This would evolve to become Web 2.0, also known as the social web, which emphasized user-generated content rather than static web pages.

Blogs and social media would also become the norm, with Facebook in 2004, and the video sharing platform YouTube starting in 2005, or even Twitter in 2006 (called twttr at the time) to mention a few.

The static and more personal web, with big website hosts like GeoCities closing down, would slowly be a thing of the past, replaced by a more homogenous web with its traffic mostly centered among a handful of big websites. Corporations would be the ones to dictate what the direction of web design would be going forward.

The most advanced web pages were built using Adobe Flash, the first technology to offer true interactivity, at the cost of performance and accessibility. It was also used to create many games that one could play within a web browser.

Slowly, web design started to emulate the modern, futuristic look that operating systems used, with a heavy use of skeuomorphism (replicating real life objects), notably with a overruse of glossy elements and reflections. The Apple website would be a prime example of this. Who doesn't remember those big glossy fake download buttons?

Of course, this wasn't popular with everyone. I've compiled a few articles that mention the new Web 2.0 look:

Stock Imagery and Advertisements

Stock Image from Asadal Design

During the early to late 2000s, HD Television and new display technologies such as Plasma TV were starting to take off in popularity, and vibrant, abstract photography and videos of nature and the ocean would often be displayed in stores and advertisements to show the full capabilities of the displays. Analog TV would also give way to Digital TV, which increased image quality for millions of people.

Asian Stock imagery featuring utopian environments would also become very popular in the early 2000s up to the early 2010s, often showing green grassy plains with tall buildings, giant globes of earth, these are the pictures that are often associated with Frutiger Aero as an aesthetic. They showed a utopian futuristic world where man, technology, and nature could co-exist in harmony, in an almost liminal space.

Asadal Designers at work

The earliest example I'm aware of goes as far back as the year 2000, with the CD-ROM collection made by Orion Press, called Tomorrow, Blue and Green, and Pure. Those images reflected the spirit of the era: future-oriented, optimistic, and full of possibility.

The most famous images are made by Asadal Design, a South Korean stock photography agency, which produced thousand of such images. Sozaijiten, a Japanese-produced collection of images by Image Navi Corporation spanning over 246 volumes on various subjects, some of which depicted nature and cities merging together, reflecting a vision of a more optimistic, eco-friendly vision of the future.

Read more about the history of Asadal Design, the biggest producer of these images, in the interview of its CEO by Yunseon Yang.

Stock images like this lost popularity with the coming of smartphone devices and minimalism, which made them look old fashioned and unpopular. They are today appreciated again by the Frutiger Aero enthusiasts community.

Frutiger Aero in Video Games

Wii (2006)

The seventh generation of video game consoles also started using Frutiger Aero-like designs for their graphical user interfaces. The Wii, aimed at more casual audiences, introduced motion controls, and a playful interface using skeuomorphism, all this accompanied by soothing music (it's worth nothing there is a sub-aesthetic dedicated to this particular asian style of Frutiger Aero, called Technozen), the PlayStation 3 and PlayStation Portable also featured their own interesting and beautiful interfaces, with Aurora Borealis backgrounds, like Windows Vista. The Xbox 360 dashboard is also worth noting.

The PlayStation Vita released in 2011 is also iconic, as it perfectly encapsulates the Frutiger Aero aesthetic with its heavy use of skeuomorphism and the floating bubbles as navigation items on the home screen. Sadly, the console was a commercial failure, which also led Sony to abandon the handheld console market.

The Wii U released on November 18, 2012, and was the last game console to feature a glossy and bubbly interface that we associate with Frutiger Aero. Due to a number of factors, it turned out to be a commercial failure, only selling 13 million units, almost leading Nintendo to bankruptcy.

The Smartphone Revolution (2007)

iPhone (2007)

In the 2007 MacWorld event Apple took the world by storm by revealing the iPhone, a phone that combined iPod, Web Browsing, and Phone, into one singular device, using only touch controls.

This was never seen in a phone before, as most used keyboards or styluses for the same functionality.

At the core of this new phone revolution was Skeuomorphism, a design concept of making items resemble their real-world counterparts, in an attempt to make the UI more intuitive for people not yet used to smartphone technology.

"The skeuomorphic design approach was a hugely debated topic inside Apple back in those days. Apple iOS SVP Scott Forstal pushed for the design direction, while legendary industrial designer Jony Ive and other Apple top-level executives were against it" [quote]

This would prove to be hugely influencial, with other phone manufacturers soon following with their own touch devices which also included skeuomorphic interfaces, notably Android phones.

The widespread use of smartphones changed the digital landscape forever, notably on the web, with Flash websites falling out of favor, soon to be replaced HTML5, this was mostly due to Adobe Flash having poor battery life on the iPhone, and Steve Job's letter "Thoughts on Flash".

In 2010, Microsoft unveiled the Windows Phone, with the first real flat design interface, which would later inspire Windows 8.

Popularization of Flat Design (2012)

Windows 8 (2012)

The flat minimalist design style was already used by Microsoft as far back as 2006, with the Zune line of devices, which were portable media players. Windows Phone in 2010 would also be the first device to use the Metro UI design, a flat tile-based interface that made its phones easier and faster to use, with a simpler and cleaner interface compared to other phones of the time.

In 2012, Microsoft was worried about the fast-growing smartphone market and how it could harm the market share of Windows in the long run. To remedy this, they decided to implement Metro UI in their new operating system: Windows 8, as an effort to make Windows a unified platform, thus eliminating the friction between phones and computers entirely.

The desktop was replaced by a Dashboard that was optimized for touch controls, but while it was great for the Surface line of tablets that Microsoft would release at the time, for PC users it was clumsy experience that utilized a lot of screen space. This approach displeased computer users who were used to a more traditional interface aimed towards mouse users.

There was an option to go back to the classic desktop by clicking one of the tiles, but the start button was absent on it. Due to backlash, Microsoft would later bring back the start button with the release of Windows 8.1 in August 2013, but by then, most people did not want to use it, and its market share never surpassed Windows 7. Despite this failure, the industry started to go towards Flat Design and drop Skeuomorphism and other elaborate designs when Apple started to shift away from that too.

Scott Forstall, who led the original software development team for the iPhone and iPad, which was a big proponent of skeuomorphism, was fired around that time, with Jonathan Paul Ive, also called Jony Ive, taking over the lead of design of iOS. Jony Ive disliked Skeumorphism due to how limiting it felt for designers, and wanted to bring innovations that Skeuomorphism just didn't allow.

Due to people having been accustomed to technology, Skeumorphism which was supposed to ease non-tech savvy people in the ecosystem, lost the whole reason of its existence for Apple. With iOS 7 which released on September 18, 2013, it was dropped entirely, being replaced with sharper, flatter icons, slimmer fonts and a more minimalistic approach.

iOS 6 to iOS 7 (2013)

Jony Ive, Apples's head of design, said this:

"the new icons feature a new palette of colors — gone are the bold, primary colors of old, replaced by modern shades and tones. Flat design is very prominent in iOS 7 — everything from the buttons to the switches to the chrome surrounding apps has been modernized and flattened. Apple says that the new design makes your phone "appear bigger" because each app makes better use of the screen real-estate available to it. The calendar, phone, messages, Game Center, and others have all lost their skeuomorphic designs and now feature clean, flat layouts. The signal bars in the upper left corner of the phone have also been replaced with a series of dots." [quote]

He also made this comment about it:

"There is a profound and enduring beauty in simplicity, in clarity, in efficiency. True simplicity is derived from so much more than just the absence of clutter and ornamentation - it's about bringing order to complexity. iOS 7 is a clear representation of these goals. It has a whole new structure that is coherent and applied across the entire system." [quote]

Due to this series of events, complex skeuomorphic interfaces lost popularity and were gradually replaced by simpler flatter versions, and less elaborate designs which are cheaper and easier to produce. Websites still using the old style were seen as old fashioned and sometimes dangerous, as skeuomorphic fake download buttons were very common on these.

CARI coins the term "Frutiger Aero" (2017)

In 2017, Sofi Xian (known mononymously as Sofi) from CARI, which stands for "Consumer Aesthetics Research Institute", an online community dedicated to developing a visual lexicon of consumer ephemera from the 1970s until now, coined the term "Frutiger Aero," it is defined as "the corporate tech aesthetic popular from approximately 2005 through 2013."

Sofi Xian (CARI co-founder)

With prominent motifs being (I quote):

  • Skeumorphism in UI/UX design
  • Glossy design
  • Frutiger/humanist sans-serif typefaces
  • Tertiary color palettes
  • Glassy/transparent materials
  • Photographs of aurora borealis
  • Bokeh photography
  • Macro photographs of grass

Namesake: Combination of Windows Vista's design guideline "Aero" and Adrian Frutiger's eponymous font "Frutiger", which originally inspired Vista's default font (Segoe UI).

Are.na / Consumer Aesthetics Research Institute Frutiger Aero

Greenwashed tech aesthetic of the 2000s-2010s. Features tertiary color palettes, stock photos of nature, glassy 3D orbs, bokeh blurs, & auroras. Glossy skeuomorphic GUIs & humanist sans serif type were popular trends (especially in the respective Aqua and Aero design guidelines for OSX and Windows). Soothing, sine-wave based ambient menu music & UI sound design. Personalized product design colorways, especially in tech peripherals like first wave smartphones & MP3 players (via the Four Colors motif). Green architecture & transportation like living rooftops, solar panels, and first wave hybrid EVs. Interior design using white plastic, glass, & bamboo materials. Documentary films & social campaigns on global warming, healthy diets, and agriculture. Common themes of harmony with nature, humanism, & climate consciousness--driven by brands with “eco-friendly” marketing campaigns, typically with the intent of laundering their image and reputations.

Revival on Social Media (2022-Present)

Image often used to represent Frutiger Aero

Frutiger Aero regained popularity around 2022 thanks to social media like TikTok, mostly with people that were kids during the early 2000s, but also younger people that were not around at the time.

New forms of the aesthetic were being made, sometimes mixing other genres like liminal spaces with Frutiger Aero. Arguably, the same thing that happened with Vaporwave is happening to Frutiger Aero, with a version that paints the past with rose tinted glasses. This can be seen with the term "The future that they promised us" being used often, which is a term that is misleading, as such a future was never promised, however, it's true that optimism for the future was present at the time, as I explained previously. The newer expression of the style is also referred to online as "Neo-Aero", as it differs with the historical variant.

Also around that time, a subreddit based around the aesthetic would be created by Ashe Lane, which at the time I'm writing this, has more than 100K members, and communities on various social media like Facebook, Twitter, and Discord. This further helped to re-popularize the aesthetic.

It will probably never become mainstream again as a design trend, however nothing prevents you to create cool artworks, wallpapers, or music; to celebrate the good old days, or simply for the love of art.
